# Cohort B: self-made investors, financiers and dealmakers

Research date: 2026-11-20. Method and honesty notes (read first):

- 36 people were searched (one or more web searches each). Only a handful had a primary or near-primary page actually fetched and read (Dalio via CNBC, Schwarzman via Entrepreneur, Druckenmiller via Macro-Ops). Everything else rests on search-result summaries of secondary sources. The search tool returns summaries, not full text, so depth per person is thin.
- The Oaktree "It's All a Big Mistake" memo (Marks) was fetched but came back as raw PDF; it could NOT be read. Marks claims below rest on search summaries only.
- Two checks were not completed: (a) whether Soros kept a real-time decision diary (the search budget ran out; NOT confirmed, do not treat as fact); (b) Bridgewater's "issue log" is confirmed only by third-party summaries (Shortform, SuperSummary, reader notes), not by the book text.
- "Not found in sources searched" means my limited search did not surface it. It does NOT mean the person lacked the habit.
- Quotes: only used where the text came from a fetched page. Search-summary wording is paraphrased.
- Wealth figures are given only where a retrieved source gave one. Otherwise "not retrieved".
- "Self-made" labels are my judgement from retrieved sources, not a rating from the sources.
- Several named people from the brief (e.g. Peterffy, Sternlicht, Loeb, Wood, Son, Zell, Ross, Icahn, Tepper, Griffin, Bogle) yielded mostly biography, little behaviour. They are included but flagged as thin.

## Warren Buffett (self-made? yes per retrieved sources, family context not retrieved; value investing / Berkshire; wealth not retrieved)
- Early years and first failures: Bought first stock at 11, took a bicycle deduction for his paper route on his 1944 tax return. Was rejected by Harvard Business School after a short interview, then applied to Columbia to study under Benjamin Graham and David Dodd. Later called the rejection pivotal. One source says Graham first turned him down even when he offered to work free, hiring him in 1954.
- Operating habits: Widely reported to spend about 80% of his working day reading and thinking. The "500 pages a day" line is unverified (Quote Investigator found no citation; a WSJ piece on Todd Combs said 500 pages a week). Began a practice in the 1989 Berkshire letter of formally listing his mistakes ("Mistakes of the First Twenty-Five Years", later headed "Mistake Du Jour"), written with humour, and argued it is good to review past mistakes before committing new ones.
- Mentors/accountability: Benjamin Graham (teacher, then employer). Charlie Munger (partner). The public annual letter is itself an accountability device.
- Biases/avoidance: Admits mistakes of omission (things seen but not acted on) via Munger's framing; admitted in the 1989 letter that buying control of Berkshire was his first mistake.
- Big losses/lapses: Handles them by publishing them. Later letters (including 2025 per Fortune) continued to describe capital-allocation errors.
- Quote: none verified.
- Sources: https://www.cnbc.com/2017/01/31/hbs-rejection-was-the-best-thing-that-ever-happened-to-warren-buffett.html ; https://quoteinvestigator.com/?p=20525 ; https://acquirersmultiple.com/2017/10/warren-buffett-mistakes-of-the-first-twenty-five-years/ ; https://fortune.com/2025/02/26/warren-buffet-investing-management-mistakes-shareholder-letter/ ; confidence: medium (secondary summaries; the 1989 letter itself not read)

- Early years/failures: Not found in sources searched (a search for 1970s partnership drawdowns returned nothing).

- Big losses: not found in sources searched.

## Ray Dalio (yes; macro hedge fund, Bridgewater; wealth not retrieved here)
- Early years/failures: Misjudged the market in the early 1980s, lost heavily, was nearly out of business and borrowed $4,000 from his father. DATE CONFLICT: Dalio's own post says 1979; most reports place it in or after 1982. He later called it a humbling that taught him to balance audacity with humility.
- Habits: Treats painful errors as something to go toward and analyse ("pain + reflection = progress"). Deliberately seeks smart people who disagree with him. Bridgewater uses radical transparency. Third-party summaries say Bridgewater has an "issue log": problems and mistakes are logged, rated for severity and assigned to a person, and concealing a mistake is treated as the worst failing. A "dot collector" tool rates people in meetings. (These two are from summaries, not the book text.)
- Mentors/accountability: Peers who disagree; a 1993 memo criticising his management style hurt him but led him to rethink how he managed.
- Biases: Describes audacity as the trait his failure forced him to balance.
- Big losses: Rebuilt after 1982 and described it as the bottom of Bridgewater, after which returns were consistently positive (secondary).
- Quote: none (the humility/audacity line is over 15 words; paraphrased).
- Sources: https://www.cnbc.com/2018/10/11/this-powerful-formula-helped-ray-dalio-build-his-18-billion-fortune.html (fetched) ; https://fortune.com/2025/08/13/billionaire-ray-dalio-broke-early-career-borrowed-4000-from-dad/ ; https://www.shortform.com/blog/bridgewater-tools/ ; confidence: high for the 1980s loss and reflection practice, medium for the issue log

## George Soros (yes; macro hedge fund, Quantum; wealth not retrieved)
- Early years: Born Budapest 1930; Nazi occupation 1944; left Hungary 1947 for the LSE; moved to New York 1956 (F.M. Mayer). Quantum Fund founded 1970 with Jim Rogers. Famous 1992 short of sterling (about $1B profit).
- Habits: Developed the theory of reflexivity. Said he used the onset of acute back pain as a signal that something was wrong in the portfolio (paraphrase of a secondary report). A real-time decision diary was NOT confirmed in my searches.
- Mentors/accountability: Mentor to Stanley Druckenmiller. Per Druckenmiller (via Macro-Ops), Soros taught that if you are extremely confident, a loss does not bother you, so exit what is not working without ego.
- Biases: Treats his body as an alarm for portfolio error. A Soros-authored essay says mistakes and distortions are unavoidable when interpreting impulses under time pressure.
- Big losses: Not found in sources searched.
- Quote: none verified directly from Soros.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Soros ; https://www.britannica.com/money/George-Soros ; https://macro-ops.com/?p=2556 ; https://macro-ops.com/?p=3440 ; confidence: medium (back-pain claim secondary)

## Jim Simons (yes; quant fund, Renaissance/Medallion; wealth not retrieved)

- Biases: Early reliance on intuition left him sick to his stomach; systematisation was the counter (reader notes).
- Big losses: Not found beyond the above.
- Quote: none.

## Stanley Druckenmiller (yes; macro; wealth: one source says over $6B, secondary)
- Early years: Early research lesson: his research director rejected a bank-sector paper he was proud of, asking what makes the stock go up and down, so he refocused on drivers of price.
- Habits: Concentrated "big bet" approach when conviction is high; timing via liquidity and technicals; avoiding large losses matters most so capital stays available. Reported to have had no losing year over about 30 years (secondary). Advice to grind up 30-40% then push hard when conviction is high.
- Mentors: George Soros. Another boss, Drelles, was technically oriented.
- Biases/lapses: In the 1987 crash he was heavily wrong, switching from net short to 130% long the day before; he recognised the error during the day and reversed. The lesson reported: flexibility and exit without ego.
- Big losses: The 2000 tech loss was not found in sources searched. No journal practice found.
- Quote: none verified in own words.
- Sources: https://macro-ops.com/?p=3440 (fetched) ; https://moneyweek.com/investments/investment-strategy/605020/stan-druckenmiller-position-size-really-matters ; confidence: medium

- Big losses: Admitted a deal loss in the 1980s that nearly brought him to tears.

## PATTERNS ACROSS THE COHORT

Number researched: 36 people received at least one search. Only about 12 had substantive, behaviour-level evidence on mistakes, habits or risk (Buffett, Munger, Dalio, Soros/Druckenmiller, Simons, Pabrai, Klarman, Schwarzman, Ackman, Thorp, Baron, Marks at summary level). For the other roughly 24, the evidence is biographical. Counts below are "documented in the entries above out of 36", not "X out of 36 do this". A missing record means my limited search missed it.

1. Early setback, rejection or dismissal then pivot: 11/36 (Buffett, Dalio, Simons, Tepper, Ross, Tudor Jones, Li Lu, Schwarzman, Bogle, Livermore, Wood).
2. Publicly admitted a specific mistake or loss and treated it as learning: 9/36 (Buffett, Dalio, Druckenmiller, Marks, Pabrai, Ackman, Schwarzman, Cooperman, Simons).
3. Large drawdown, collapse or closure documented: 10/36 (Dalio, Robertson, Ackman, Paulson, Livermore, Wood, Son, Cooperman, Simons, Burry). Responses diverged: Dalio rebuilt, Robertson returned capital, Ackman exited positions, Livermore did not recover.
4. Downside-first or sizing rules stated: 7/36 (Klarman, Druckenmiller, Tudor Jones, Thorp, Munger, Pabrai on the 4.9% stake, Ackman on not cutting two big positions).
5. Named mentor or adviser relationship: 7/36 (Buffett-Graham, Li Lu-Munger, Druckenmiller-Soros, Tudor Jones-Tullis, Robertson-cubs, Schwarzman-Peterson, Bogle-Morgan).
6. High-volume reading or direct research: 5/36 (Buffett, Munger, Lynch, Naval, Baron).
7. Self-awareness of bias or emotion with a counter-device: 6/36 (Soros backache signal, Munger's 25 causes, Simons' systematisation, Dalio's humility, Schwarzman's embarrassment, Tudor Jones' emotional-control model).
8. Formal written structure for errors or decisions: 4/36 (Buffett annual mistakes section, Munger misjudgment checklist, Pabrai checklist, Dalio issue log via secondary sources). A decision journal specifically was documented for 0/36 in my searches. Soros's diary is unconfirmed.

Important: pre-mortems were not found under that name for anyone. Munger's inversion and Klarman's downside-first approach are analogues only.

Contradictions:
- Concentrate vs diversify: Druckenmiller and Baron hold large, concentrated positions; Thorp (half-Kelly), Klarman and Bogle favour caution or breadth. Ackman's Valeant loss and Paulson's show concentration blow-ups.
- Cut losses without ego (Soros/Druckenmiller) vs hold through drawdowns if the thesis holds (Baron on Tesla; Pabrai averaged down, criticised).
- Learn from pain (Dalio) vs not formalising a post-mortem (Ackman said Gotham's lessons were never formally written up).
- Own the loss publicly (Buffett, Ackman's 2017 letter) vs defending (Cooperman denied the SEC allegations).
- Reflexive instinct (early Simons) failed; systematic models won. Buffett works largely from judgement and reading.

Caveats:
- Survivorship bias is severe. Everyone here won big. Livermore is the only documented ruin, and Robertson, Paulson, Ackman and Wood show decline. We cannot see the people who kept the same habits and failed.
- Source quality is poor. Mostly secondary summaries, blog/Substack notes, Wikipedia, and wealth-media articles. Several well-known "habits" are folklore with disputed origins (Buffett's "500 pages a day"; Livermore via a fictionalised book; Dalio's loan date; Naval's quotes curated by a third party).
- Self-reporting bias: memoirs, letters and interviews are curated; mistakes shown are often the ones that make a good story.
- "Self-made" is fuzzy: Griffin, Burry, Loeb and Ross each had family capital or connections. None of these figures can be separated from luck and timing.

## WHAT THIS MEANS FOR A MENTOR

For a 21-year-old CS student with a remote infra job, his own product and a goal of becoming a self-made millionaire. Each point cites the evidence above and flags how strong that evidence is.

1. Run a weekly, written "mistakes" review as a fixed ritual. Buffett's annual mistakes list and Marks' memo on mistakes (summary level) support reviewing errors regularly; the point of the exercise is the process, not the outcome. Evidence level: medium. Treat the weekly review as the core of the system.
2. Log misses the same day, in a short structured entry (what, severity, root cause, owner). Bridgewater's issue log (secondary) and Dalio's "pain plus reflection" support it. Make the cost of logging near zero so the habit survives a bad week. A daily note that says "nothing missed" should be questioned, since the evidence says concealment is the failure.
3. Use a short pre-commitment checklist rather than a long one. Pabrai's checklist (about 97 questions, 15-20 minutes) was built from other people's mistakes. For this user, build 8-12 questions from his own logged misses and read them before each commitment, not as a generic list. Munger's 25 misjudgment causes work as the source list.
4. Run a pre-mortem-style step before each non-trivial commitment, labelled as an analogue. Nobody in the research used the word, but Munger's inversion and Klarman's risk-first framing support asking "how does this fail, what is the downside" before "what is the upside". Ask for the likely objections too (Schwarzman's unprepared pitches).
5. Write a kill criterion for each commitment at the time it is made. Druckenmiller via Soros: exit what is not working without ego; Ackman admitted not cutting two big positions; Pabrai's 4.9% sizing mistake. For the product: a date and a measurable bar, so abandoning is a logged decision not a drift.
6. Keep a decision log (what, why, expected result, date to check), then compare against outcomes. No investor in this research was documented keeping a decision journal, and Soros's diary is unconfirmed, so this is inference from the mistakes-log evidence, not a documented practice. Judge the process separately from the outcome (Marks, summary level).
7. Promptly write post-mortems for big misses. Ackman's Gotham lessons were never written up and he repeated pattern errors (Business Insider framing, secondary), while Buffett wrote his up annually. Rule: a post-mortem within a set number of days of any missed commitment.
8. Protect downside and runway first. Klarman, Tudor Jones, Thorp and Livermore (the cautionary case) point to survival as the first rule. For a student with a salary, the job is the margin of safety; do not risk it on the product until the product has evidence. Position sizing for time and money should be explicit.
9. Make learning volume a tracked commitment, but with caution. Buffett, Munger, Lynch, Naval and Baron all show heavy reading or direct research, yet the figures are folklore ("500 pages a day" is unverified). Track hours and notes produced, not page counts, and tie reading to a decision it will inform.
10. Give permission to say "this is not my edge" and to step back. Robertson returned capital rather than invest in a market he did not understand; Buffett's mistakes of omission cut the other way, so pair the permission with a log of what was passed on and why, reviewed later.
11. Find a named person who will see the log. Mentor or adviser relationships appear in 7/36 (Buffett-Graham, Druckenmiller-Soros, Li Lu-Munger). Dalio sought people who disagree. The bot can hold the log but a human reviewer for the weekly review would add real accountability. Evidence level: medium.
12. Be honest about the evidence ceiling. This cohort shows what winners say and do; it cannot show which habits caused the wins. Use the system to test habits on his own data after 8-12 weeks, and drop any that do not change his behaviour. Everything above is a hypothesis to try, not a proven recipe.

Implication for the daily commitment-and-review system, in short: daily = commit, check against the short checklist, log any miss in one line the same day. Weekly = mistakes review plus decision-log review against outcomes. Per commitment = kill criterion plus downside question before starting. After any significant miss = written post-mortem within a few days. Monthly = revise the checklist from his own misses.
