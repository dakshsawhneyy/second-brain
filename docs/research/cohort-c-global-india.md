# Cohort C: non-US self-made founders and billionaires (India, Asia, emerging markets, others)

Method and honesty note. Research was done via web search only (about 40 searches, results returned as summaries with source URLs). I did NOT open and read full primary documents or books for any person. Every claim below is traceable to a URL in that person's Sources line, but many sources are secondary (Wikipedia, Business Today, YourStory, DNA India, aggregator "success story" sites). Where a detail was disputed between sources I say so. Where I found nothing (very often for daily habits, mentors, accountability), I write "not found" rather than guess. Dates and figures are as reported, not independently verified. Search-tool output sometimes noted that my own query premises were unsupported (e.g. Adani kidnapping, Ren Zhengfei firing, Murthy IIT rejection); those premises are not repeated here.

People covered: 39 individuals in 38 entries (the Bansals share one entry). Not researched at all: none of the requested names were skipped, but depth varies from decent (Murthy, Nithin Kamath, Ma, Kamprad) to thin (Zhang Yiming, Ortega, Ibrahim, Byju, Son).

## N. R. Narayana Murthy (self-made? yes, with spousal seed money; IT services/Infosys; Infosys market cap reported in the lakhs of crores of Rs, personal net worth about Rs 38,000 crore per one outlet)
- Early years: Worked at IIM Ahmedabad (research associate, later chief systems programmer), took a job in Paris on a team that built an operating system in the early 1970s, returned to India in 1976 and set up Softronics, which failed within roughly 1 to 1.5 years (sources differ). Then worked at Patni Computer Systems in Pune. In 1981 he and six others founded Infosys with Rs 10,000 supplied by his wife Sudha Murty (described as loan or contribution, accounts differ). Sudha said she took a risk partly because Softronics had failed.
- Habits: Says he worked about 14 hours a day, 6.5 days a week, arriving around 6:20 to 6:30 am and leaving around 8:30 pm for decades; claimed 85-90 hour weeks until retirement in 2011. (Self-reported, and he later said these are "deeply personal decisions", not a prescription.) No wake-time claim found.
- Mentors/accountability: Spouse as first financier and a risk partner; six co-founders. No named mentor found in these sources.
- Comfort-zone/pressures: Left a salaried job after a failed first venture and went again; the second time with a co-founder team. Family pressure details not found.
- Setbacks: Softronics shutdown (above). Also reported that Azim Premji's Wipro rejected him for a job (Zee News; low confidence).
- Quote (verified in press): "I don't believe in work-life balance" (Entrepreneur India report).
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N._R._Narayana_Murthy ; https://www.businesstoday.in/amp/latest/corporate/story/only-investment-i-have-done-is-rs-10000-sudha-murty-says-took-a-little-bit-of-risk-on-narayana-murthy-421600-2024-03-15 ; https://www.entrepreneur.com/business-news/infosys-cofounder-78-doesnt-believe-in-work-life-balance/483158 ; https://businesstoday.in/latest/corporate/story/630-am-to-830-pm-for-40-years-narayana-murthy-clears-air-on-70-hour-workweek-debate-461468-2025-01-21 ; https://businesstoday.in/latest/corporate/story/used-to-be-in-office-at-620-am-infosys-co-founder-narayana-murthy-says-he-worked-for-85-90-hours-in-initial-years-408800-2023-12-09 ; confidence: medium-high

## PATTERNS ACROSS THE COHORT

Actual N: 39 people in 38 entries (the Bansals combined). Counts below are out of 38 entries and are tallies of what my sources happened to mention, so they undercount anything journalists rarely report (habits, mentors). Several people are counted in more than one pattern.

Recurring behaviours and facts, strongest first:
1. Worked in an employer, industry or hands-on trade before founding, so they brought domain knowledge: about 20/38 (Murthy, Nadar, Shaw, Falguni, Bhavish, Deepinder, Kunal, Bansals, Vembu, Nithin, Dhirubhai, Ma, Pony Ma, Zhang, Lei, Ren, Mittal, Ortega, Ibrahim, Dyson). Almost nobody in this cohort went from zero experience to a big company.
2. Founded with a partner, brother, cousin, spouse-funder or core team rather than alone: about 13/38 (Murthy, Nadar, Nithin, Nikhil, Bansals, Cannon-Brookes, Pony Ma, Goyal, Bhavish, Kunal, Vembu, Ren, Dhirubhai).
3. Started with very small or self-owned capital, no VC at first: about 10/38 (Murthy Rs 10,000; Shaw Rs 10,000; Nadar; Nithin; Nikhil; Vembu; Cannon-Brookes $10,000 on a credit card; Ren CNY 21,000; Dangote family loan; Kamprad with a gift from father).
4. Documented early rejection or failed first attempt, then continued: about 9/38 (Ma, Murthy, Nithin, Shaw, Bansals, Branson, Dyson, Masiyiwa, Musk 2008).
5. Left formal schooling early or never finished: about 8/38 (Adani, Nikhil, Ritesh, Branson, Li, Ortega, Damani, Premji interrupted). Counter-pattern: elite credentials are just as common, about 15/38 have IIT/IIM/Stanford/Berkeley/Cornell-class degrees (Vembu, Bhavish, Deepinder, Bansals, Falguni, Murthy is listed on IIT Kanpur alumni pages in the search results, though I did not read the page, Premji, Tata, Son, Colin Huang, Zhang, Lei, Musk, Cannon-Brookes). There is no single education pattern.
6. Family business, family money or named family mentor behind them: about 7 direct (Premji, Mittal, Dangote, Tata, Damani, Kamprad, Murthy via wife) plus 3 exposure-only (Falguni, Ritesh, Slim). The "self-made" label is generous.
7. Documented long hours or double-shift stretches: about 5/38 (Murthy 14h/6.5 days, Nithin night call centre, Nikhil night shift, Li 16-hour days, Dyson 5 years of prototyping).
8. Frugality or low-profile living: about 5/38 (Premji, Kamprad, Damani, Ortega, Slim ledger discipline).
9. Started a first company by 25: about 8/38 (Ritesh, Kamprad, Branson, Cannon-Brookes, Vijay Shekhar, Shaw, Bhavish, Musk). Started at 35+: 5/38 (Falguni 50, Ren 43, Ortega 39, Murthy 35, Ibrahim about 52). Starting late is not rare.
10. A named mentor or structured accountability: only about 4/38 clearly sourced (Tata/JRD, Dangote/family, Slim/father, Ritesh/Thiel network). Mentor/advisor data is mostly absent from the press I could reach.

Finding that matters: daily-habit evidence is the weakest category in the dataset. Only 5-8 of 38 have any sourced routine at all. I cannot honestly claim that routines drive outcomes here.

For people starting without capital or connections (the user's case):
- Nithin and Nikhil Kamath, Murthy, Shaw, Cannon-Brookes, Vembu, Ren are the closest analogues: tiny capital, no VC, bootstrapped, often with a partner, and (Nithin, Nikhil) a boring paid job funding the real work. Kamath's own framing is that bootstrapping was forced, not a principle.
- They were not "no experience" founders. Most spent years in a job or trade first. Murthy's first venture failed, then he returned to employment before founding Infosys.
- Where evidence on constraint is strongest: Shaw (no bank loans, found one lender), Vijay Shekhar (Hindi-medium schooling, struggled with English), Kunal Shah (worked at 15, middle-class), Nithin (blew up trading capital twice).
- Social capital existed even where money did not: spouse (Murthy), brother (Kamath), cousin (Ambani), co-founder (Cannon-Brookes). Nobody in this set clearly did it fully alone.

Contradictions:
- Hours: Murthy and Li report extreme hours; Cannon-Brookes, Nadar and others report no such thing. Hours are self-reported and survivor-told.
- Education: dropouts (Adani, Branson, Nikhil) and Ivy/IIT graduates (Vembu, Bansals) both succeed.
- Risk: Murthy and Shaw left or skipped stable paths; Binny said Amazon was riskier than starting Flipkart; Falguni left a safe top job at 50. But Dhirubhai and Ortega built slowly from jobs.
- Funding: bootstrap (Zerodha, Zoho, Atlassian) versus heavy VC then fall (Byju's) versus heavy VC then win (Flipkart, Ola).
- Ma's narrative of persistence is self-told; counts of failures differ by source.

Caveats (survivorship and source quality):
- This is survivorship bias: the list is billionaires and household names; we do not see the many similar people who failed.
- Failure counts and anecdotes (Ma's rejections, Dyson's 5,127, Murthy's hours) are repeated and polished by the people themselves.
- Many sources are low-quality aggregators (DNA, StartupTalky, slideshows, Wikipedia mirrors); I flagged those entries low confidence. Dates and amounts frequently conflict.
- I read search summaries, not full interviews or books. A proper pass for Murthy, Nithin Kamath and Shaw should read the original podcasts (Z47, Nikhil Kamath's WTF podcast, Ranveer Show) and Murthy's Computer History Museum oral histories (links appeared in the results: archive.computerhistory.org).
- Byju, Kamprad and Son are included partly as cautionary or character-flaw cases; do not treat as models.
- Chinese and African founders are thinly documented in English and often through state-friendly or press-release sources.

## WHAT THIS MEANS FOR A MENTOR

For a middle-class Indian student aiming at a remote infra/DevOps/SRE job abroad plus an own product.

1. Do the job first, on purpose; treat it as the funding and the apprenticeship. 20/38 had prior employment or trade before founding; Murthy returned to a job after Softronics failed; Nithin funded trading with a night call-centre job; Cannon-Brookes ran on tiny salaries. Mentor stance: the SRE job is not a detour. Push for strong employer-side learning (on-call, incident reviews, cost, reliability) and an expiry-free side product.
2. Start small and keep expenses low. 10/38 began with tiny capital; Cannon-Brookes launched Jira on a roughly $10,000 credit card. Mentor stance: first product should be buildable on free tiers (the project's free-only rule fits), aimed at paying customers early. Nithin said bootstrapping was forced; Zerodha was reportedly profitable in year one.
3. Find a partner. 13/38 had a co-founder, sibling, spouse or team. Mentor should regularly ask "who is your Nikhil / Binny / Farquhar" and treat solo-only plans with suspicion, because accountability structures are almost invisible in the evidence and a partner is the closest thing to one.
4. Expect and plan for early failure; weight comeback behaviour. 9/38 had documented early failure (Murthy, Nithin twice, Dyson). Mentor tracks: "what did you ship, what did you learn, what is the next smaller attempt" after a miss, not shame. Nithin's response to blow-ups was a boring job to rebuild capital; use that template for money lapses.
5. Do not let the job-vs-business choice be binary. Murthy's route was job, failed venture, job, then Infosys; Falguni started at about 50; Ibrahim at about 52; Ren at 43. 5/38 started after 35. Mentor can tell the student there is no deadline pressure of the "now or never" type, and that a late start is common. Do not promise $1M by 25; no one in this cohort supports that timeline as typical (only Musk, Ritesh, Branson, Kamprad started very young, with different circumstances).
6. Family and social pressure: the evidence is thin, so be honest. Direct evidence of pressure and breakthrough is scarce (Kamath's mother flushing his phones, one outlet; Murthy's wife taking a risk on him; Shaw's gender barriers at banks). The consistent pattern is a trusted family person or partner financing or vouching. Mentor suggestion: help the student get one family member to buy in with a small, time-boxed, explicit experiment (for example "6 months, a fixed hours budget, a job stays") rather than fighting the whole family.
7. Do not copy the hours narrative. Murthy's 14-hour days and Li's 16-hour days are self-told and survivor-biased, and Murthy himself said they are personal choices. For a student holding a job and a product, use a sustainable weekly time budget and track consistency over intensity. The evidence cannot prove that hours cause success.
8. Credentials are not required but domain depth is. Both dropouts and IIT graduates appear. Mentor emphasis: prove skills with public work (infra projects, incident write-ups), not a degree race, while staying realistic that remote jobs abroad often screen on experience.
9. Be cautious about scaling and governance. Byju's arc (valuation to insolvency, regulatory and legal proceedings), Son's Vision Fund losses, Tata's Nano all show growth ahead of discipline. Mentor should include a monthly check: cash, obligations, compliance, and what would break first. This is a cautionary lesson from news reports, not a trait I could verify in his daily habits.
10. Use frugality as a default. Premji, Kamprad, Damani, Ortega, Slim (ledger at age ~12) point to money habits. Mentor can have the student keep a simple monthly ledger, and use cost reduction as a designed feature of the product.
11. Weekly review of what was done versus committed, because documented habits are rare in the evidence and public narratives are unreliable. The mentor system should rely on the student's own data. Treat founders' stories as hypotheses, not prescriptions; label confidence when quoting any of them to the student.
12. Plan a second look at primary sources. Before the mentor quotes any figure from this file to the student, check the original (interviews, oral histories). Priority reads: Nithin Kamath (Z47 and YourStory), Murthy (Computer History Museum oral history), Shaw (Business Today 2023 interview).
