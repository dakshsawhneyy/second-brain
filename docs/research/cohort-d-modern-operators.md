# Cohort D: modern self-made operators, bootstrappers, mentors, solo software founders

Researched 2026-11-20. Method: about 35 WebSearch calls plus 4 page fetches (levels.io 12-startups post, TechCrunch and Russell Reynolds pieces on Bill Campbell, patio11's 2010 year-in-review). The WebSearch tool returns summaries, not full pages, so most entries rest on secondary reporting, not on the person's own text. I did NOT read books, full podcast transcripts or most primary blogs. The Hormozi transcript fetch failed (HTTP 403).

Confidence labels: high = confirmed by a primary or reputable source this session; medium = consistent across secondary sources; low = single weak source or my background knowledge. Anything marked "(background)" is from my own prior knowledge and was not verified in this session. Revenue and net-worth figures are mostly self-reported or aggregator numbers. "Quote" lines are only included where a search result carried the exact wording; otherwise "no verified quote". Treat all quotes as medium at best because they came via search summaries.

Not researched individually: Y Combinator founders as a class (only the YC mentors Graham, Livingston, Tan, Altman are covered). Shaan Puri has only a thin entry. Gaps are stated per entry.

## Bill Campbell (the Trillion Dollar Coach) (self-made? n/a; coach to Jobs, Page, Schmidt, Bezos and others)
- Path: Left Kodak for Apple in 1983 (TechCrunch); later executive coach and board member at Google, Apple and others (background).
- How he coached (verified from TechCrunch and Russell Reynolds): first question "Are you coachable?" Screened for honesty, humility, perseverance/hard work, constant openness to learning. Asked diagnostic questions: "What are we trying to fix?", "What can we do?", how much is people, technology, process. Asked operational questions: how do you run your staff meeting, when is the agenda prepared, how do you expect people to participate. Ben Horowitz recalled he "would never tell me what to do" and kept asking questions. Feedback: be "relentlessly honest and candid"; give as soon as possible; negative feedback privately, paired with caring (RR). Praise in public (a book-summary claim). Standard 1:1 started with personal chatter, then performance, peers, teams, innovation, with topics kept to a handful (summaries of the book). Mentee makes the final decision. Team first; "One asshole can spoil a solid team"; CEO breaks ties.
- Mentors/accountability structures: regular 1:1s, board seats, a standing coaching relationship, not courses.
- Discipline: not framed as willpower; framed as trust, candor, and a team.
- Sources: https://techcrunch.com/2016/01/12/lessons-from-bill-campbell-silicon-valleys-secret-executive-coach ; https://www.russellreynolds.com/en/insights/reports-surveys/are-you-coachable-insights-from-a-trillion-dollar-coach ; https://www.shortform.com/blog/one-on-one-meeting-template/ . Confidence: high for the quoted items, medium for the 1:1 structure (book summaries). Caveat: he coached executives already running large organizations; hindsight and hagiography (the book is written by admirers).

## PATTERNS ACROSS THE COHORT

Actual N: 44 entries (about 25 with meaningful behavioural evidence; the rest are thin or mostly outcome claims). Counts below are my own tally of entries where the behaviour is documented in this file, not a statistical measure.

Recurring behaviours, strongest first:
1. Volume before polish or optimisation: Hormozi (100/day), Levels (12 in 12), Marc Lou (many small launches), Cardone (8-12 follow-ups), MrBeast (obsessive iteration), Vassallo (many small bets), Gary V (daily content). About 7.
2. Public accountability or transparency (income reports, build logs, year-in-reviews, public vlogs): Levels, Flynn, patio11, Barry, Yongfook, Marc Lou, Vassallo, Gary V, Welsh (revenue lines), Lavingia (failure essay). About 10. Note: for creators this is also marketing.
3. Talk to or sell to customers by hand early, before scaling: Graham/YC, Tan, Hormozi, Blakely (cold sales), Daymond (trade shows), Corcoran, Cardone, Ramit (pay-first test), Tobi (sold to merchants who asked). About 9.
4. Keep income while building (day job, consulting, or other revenue) then jump: Blakely, Daymond, patio11 (consulting funds runway), Welsh, Flynn (laid off), Vassallo (saved), Yongfook. About 7.
5. Reframe failure and rejection as information or as the cost of trying: Blakely, Corcoran, Lavingia, Levels (one-month test), Herjavec, Ferriss (26 of 27 rejections). About 6.
6. Fixed, boring commitments rather than motivation: Jocko (consistent wake time), Goggins (daily mirror), Rule of 100, Gary V "religious" about daily content, Walling/others implied. About 5, with a caveat that most come from two discipline authors.
7. Build for a specific, narrow audience: Arvid (ESL teachers), Flynn (LEED test takers), Graham (perfect for one user), Codie (boring businesses), Barry (creators). About 5.

What indie/bootstrapped founders show about timelines and daily output:
- Typical timelines are years, not months: Yongfook about 3 years to about $600K ARR (and a team of 7); Barry about 5 years to $1M MRR with a visible dip in year 2; patio11 started in 2006 and sold in 2015, with about $26K profit on Bingo Card Creator in 2010; Arvid about 2 years to $55K MRR; Marc Lou about 5 years of failed startups before the first big hit; Levels about 70 projects, about 4 that made money.
- Reported $1M+ solo: Marc Lou ($1.03M in 2025), Tony Dinh (over $1M/year, 4-hour days), Levels (claims vary, from $200K/year in 2015 to far higher claims now), Vassallo (about $1M in 3 years). All are public self-reports on aggregator sites; none audited.
- Daily output is modest and consistent, not heroic: patio11 about six hours a day, five days a week on the new product (after earlier 90-hour weeks while juggling consulting); Tony Dinh about four hours a day at maturity; Yongfook 50:50 coding and marketing; Koe "2 hours writing a day" (marketing claim). Early stage usually means much more.
- The pattern is "ship, measure demand in weeks, kill or double down": Levels' one-month test; Barry's 6-month $5K MRR target.

Contradictions:
- Calm versus extreme: Fried/DHH (no goals, 6-week cycles, fewer hours) and Dinh (4 hours) against Cardone (10x everything), MrBeast (obsession), Hormozi (volume), patio11's own 90-hour weeks. Plausible reconciliation: intensity early, sustainable cadence later, and the calm voices already have profitable firms.
- Quit-and-leap versus keep-the-job: Altman and Thiel (drop out, all-in) against Blakely, Daymond and patio11 (kept income while building).
- Scale small versus scale big: Lavingia, Fried/DHH and Walling (profitable, small) against YC (venture scale). Lavingia moved from the second to the first after a very visible failure.
- Motivation: Jocko says do not rely on it; Goggins builds emotional fuel (cookie jar, anger); Corcoran and O'Leary describe rejection and haters as motivation.
- Fail-fast (Levels) versus patience (Barry's 5 years, patio11's 9). Both are true: kill ideas fast, keep a chosen business long.

Survivorship, hype and source-quality caveats:
- Almost all of these are winners picked because they are famous; the denominators (people who ran 100 outreaches a day or 12 startups and got nothing) are invisible. Levels' own number (about 4 of 70) is the rare honest denominator.
- Many sell courses, coaching, supplements or sales training (Hormozi, Cardone, Welsh, Koe, Ramit, Flynn, Codie, Ferriss, Jocko, Gary V). Their advice also doubles as marketing; income "proof" is often self-reported.
- Revenue and net-worth figures come from aggregators, promotional podcast copy and AI-generated profile sites (celebritynetworth, mabumbe, yespress, "Generated by AI" pages). I discounted these.
- Origin stories are polished retellings; several details (Blakely's $5,000 and dates, Daymond's dates, Hormozi's gym names) conflict across sources.
- Disclosed controversy: MrBeast's leaked guide came amid reporting on workplace culture and unsafe sets. Thiel Fellowship success is selection-biased.
- I did not read most primary books or full transcripts. Thin entries (Shaan Puri, Koe, Walling, Tony Dinh, Vassallo, Cardone, Livingston) should not be quoted as findings.

## WHAT THIS MEANS FOR A MENTOR

Context: a 21-year-old CS student with a remote infra job and an own product. These are recommendations; each cites the evidence above, and I mark where evidence is thin.

1. Make the mentor's first question Campbell's: "Are you coachable?" Set coachability as an explicit contract (honest, humble, will work, keeps learning) and re-check it when he bristles at feedback. Evidence: Campbell, Russell Reynolds.
2. Ask questions before giving answers. Use Campbell's diagnostic set ("What are we trying to fix?", "What can we do?", is it a people, technology or process problem?) and operational ones ("how do you run your week, when do you plan it?"), and let him decide. Evidence: Campbell via TechCrunch; Horowitz's recollection.
3. Give hard feedback fast, privately, candidly and with visible care. If the mentor chats first, note Campbell's 1:1 started with personal topics. Never wait for the weekly review to say a commitment was missed. Evidence: Campbell (Russell Reynolds, TechCrunch).
4. Run a short, fixed 1:1 or weekly review with a handful of topics: performance, relationships, the product, one risk. Adjust the style by task-relevant maturity: close and directive on tasks he has never done (e.g., sales outreach), hands-off on tasks he has mastered (infra). Evidence: Campbell 1:1 format (book summaries), Grove (HOM summaries). Medium confidence; both sources are second-hand.
5. Track output, not hours, and use leverage thinking: review last week's calendar and classify low, medium, high leverage. For a student with a job, only a few hours matter, so spend them on distribution, not features. Evidence: Grove leverage; Yongfook's 50:50 code and marketing; patio11's 6-hour days.
6. Set minimum daily or weekly volume quotas on customer-facing actions (outreach, conversations, public updates), with a fixed time window (Jocko: a consistent time matters more than the exact time). Evidence: Hormozi's rule of 100 (marketing-adjacent), Cardone, Gary V, Jocko. Start low: a quota he can sustain beside the job, and increase it; do not copy Hormozi's 100 as-is.
7. Build in public with real numbers. Weekly log, monthly public post; failure essays count. This is accountability that costs nothing. Evidence: Levels, patio11's year-in-reviews, Flynn, Barry, Lavingia. Caveat: for creators it is also marketing, so do not turn it into performance.
8. Set kill criteria and time boxes up front: for each experiment, a date and a demand signal (Levels: one month; Barry: 6 months to $5K MRR). Treat a miss as information (Blakely, Corcoran), not as character verdict. Evidence: Levels, Barry, Blakely.
9. Plan honestly for timelines of 2 to 5 years. Show him the actual curves: Yongfook about 3 years to $600K ARR, Barry about 5 years to $1M MRR with a dip, patio11 $26K profit after 4 years. Protect the day job as runway (Blakely, Daymond, patio11 all kept income). Do not present Levels' or Marc Lou's right-tail results as base rates. Evidence: indie timelines above.
10. Prepare for motivation fading: the default response is a smaller committed action at a fixed time, not a pep talk (Jocko). Add a "cookie jar" log of past wins (Goggins), but treat the 40% rule as a heuristic. Review honestly when he stalls, as patio11's page admits a six-month stall, then restart small. Evidence: Jocko, Goggins, patio11.
11. Handle the marketing noise: the mentor should tell him which of these voices sell courses and label advice from them as hypotheses to test. Offer a few strong, narrow sources (Graham's essay, patio11, Campbell/Grove) rather than the whole loud list. Evidence: source-quality section above.
12. Guard against burnout and the hustle trap: the 90-hour and "obsession" norms come from people who survived and from contexts with team support. Cap load, review sleep and health, and note Fried/DHH and Dinh's lower-hour sustainability once the product earns. Evidence: patio11's 90-hour period, MrBeast controversy, Fried/DHH. Note: the project's private-data rule means any such health notes stay local.

Gaps worth a follow-up: primary transcripts for Hormozi, Naval, Welsh, Koe and Walling; Grove's documented mentoring of founders; the concrete content of the Campbell/Schmidt 1:1 checklist from the book itself; verified first-year revenue histories for Marc Lou and Tony Dinh.
